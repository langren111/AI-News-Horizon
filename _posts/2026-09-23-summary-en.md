The strongest discovered agent matches or exceeds a human-engineered production research agent on four held-out benchmarks, including out-of-distribution physics-based weather forecasting. This is a rare concrete demonstration of recursive self-improvement, a long-theorized capability with paradigm-shifting potential for AI R&D. It suggests AI research agents could counter diminishing returns on R&D spending by autonomously improving their own research efficiency. AIDE^2 proposes code changes, benchmarks modified versions of itself on AI R&D tasks, and keeps only changes that perform best on hidden evaluations. Notably, reward hacking fell from 55% to 32% during the run even though the loop never explicitly optimized for it, and gains generalized to machine learning engineering, heuristic algorithm engineering, and weather forecasting.

Prior work includes agents like Voyager and Gödel Agent, but most systems rely on a fixed underlying model rather than improving the agent's own scaffolding code. AIDE^2 targets the agent's research efficiency specifically, treating its own code as the object of optimization.

## [Trail of Bits Critiques SAML as a Fractal of Bad Design](https://blog.trailofbits.com/2026/09/21/saml-a-fractal-of-bad-design/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Trail of Bits published a critical analysis titled "SAML: A fractal of bad design," arguing that the Security Assertion Markup Language is fundamentally flawed and prone to security pitfalls. The post sparked extensive community discussion with real-world horror stories about XML signature validation and authentication bypasses. SAML remains widely deployed in enterprise single sign-on, so its design flaws directly affect the security of countless organizations and identity providers. The discussion highlights why engineers should understand these pitfalls and consider alternatives like OIDC while acknowledging SAML's entrenched enterprise role. Commenters noted that early C implementations of XML signature validation would accept signatures checked against attacker-controlled HMAC passwords or even web PKI TLS keys, and that XML Signature Wrapping (XSW) attacks exploit the gap between what is signed and what is processed. Others argued that canonicalization should be avoided entirely by validating signatures over the exact received blob before decoding.

hackernews · aray07 · Sep 22, 18:57 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49806335)

**Background**: SAML is an XML-based open standard for exchanging authentication and authorization data between an identity provider and a service provider, with its last major revision in 2005. It predates modern federated identity protocols like OIDC and relies on XML signatures, whose complexity has led to recurring vulnerabilities such as XML Signature Wrapping. These flaws typically arise when service providers improperly validate or process SAML responses.

## [No-Persona LLM Baseline Beats Synthetic Persona Panels at Predicting Real Clicks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25010) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A new sim-to-real validity study (arXiv:2609.25010) using the Upworthy Research Archive found that a no-persona zero-shot LLM baseline predicts real headline click-through markedly better (Kendall τ = 0.361, top-1 accuracy 49.2%) than a ten-persona panel grounded in the real audience's demographics (τ = 0.084, top-1 34.6%), with non-overlapping confidence intervals. The result replicated across three independent Upworthy splits, held in direction on a different-domain news dataset, and was robust to seed, prompt phrasing, and model choice across three Gemini tiers plus OpenAI gpt-4.1. This is a notable negative result that challenges a widely held assumption in LLM-based marketing and social simulation: that demographic-grounded synthetic personas improve prediction of real audience behavior. For practitioners using LLMs to pre-test copy or forecast engagement, the finding suggests that simply asking the model about a typical reader is not only cheaper but more accurate than elaborate persona role-play, and it raises broader questions about the validity of persona-based simulation in social science research. Ground-truth reliability is the binding constraint: most A/B tests in the archive have no statistically distinguishable winner, so validity could only be measured on the reliable subset (n = 399). The authors argue that asking the model directly taps an accurate population-level prior, whereas forcing it to role-play specific personas injects bias and noise; all numbers regenerate from a public, artifact-first replication package.

rss · ArXiv CS.AI · Sep 23, 04:00

**Background**: The Upworthy Research Archive is a public dataset of thousands of real headline A/B tests run on shared live traffic, with measured click-through rates, making it a rare source of ground truth for audience-response prediction. Synthetic personas are LLM prompts that condition the model on a demographic or psychographic profile so it can 'stand in' for a real user; marketers and researchers increasingly use them to pre-test copy and simulate survey or focus-group responses. Sim-to-real validity studies compare such simulated predictions against real-world outcomes to check whether the simulation actually generalizes.

## [Lean Pool: An AI-Maintained Archive of Formalized Mathematics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25199) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A new arXiv paper (2609.25199v1) introduces Lean Pool, a repository of formalized mathematics that is grown, maintained, and optimized entirely by AI agents. Unlike prior formalization efforts driven by human contributors, Lean Pool positions autonomous agents as the primary curators of the library. If AI agents can reliably grow and optimize a formal math library, it could dramatically accelerate the formalization of mathematics and reduce the human labor bottleneck in projects like Lean's mathlib. This also creates a feedback loop where better formal libraries improve automated theorem-proving agents, benefiting both formal verification and AI-driven mathematical research. The abstract is extremely brief and does not specify the agent architecture, the size or scope of the current repository, or how correctness and consistency are guaranteed. It is also unclear whether human review is involved at any stage or how contributions are validated against Lean's kernel.

rss · ArXiv CS.AI · Sep 23, 04:00

**Background**: Lean is a functional programming language and interactive theorem prover developed with involvement from Microsoft Research, used to write proofs that a computer can check for correctness. Formalized mathematics means encoding mathematical definitions and proofs in such a system so every step is machine-verified, as in libraries like mathlib. AI agents for theorem proving, such as recent minimal Lean 4 provers, iteratively refine proofs and search existing libraries, and Lean Pool appears to apply that agentic approach to maintaining the library itself.
