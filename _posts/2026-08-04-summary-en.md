## [SARE: Quantifying Step-Wise Reasoning Effort in LLM Chain-of-Thought](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28674) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Researchers propose Step-Aware Reasoning Energy (SARE), a geometric framework using Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) between Gram matrices of adjacent transformer layers to quantify computational effort at each chain-of-thought (CoT) step. Across six reasoning benchmarks and three open-weight LLMs, SARE reveals non-uniform energy allocation with phase-like transitions and lower energy at critical junctions in incorrect trajectories. This work addresses an open interpretability challenge by moving beyond output-level or trajectory-level metrics to expose step-wise reasoning dynamics. It could improve confidence estimation and error detection in LLMs, with implications for model debugging, safety, and efficient resource allocation during inference. SARE uses CKA between Gram matrices of token hidden states across adjacent layers, capturing inter-token relational structure without requiring eigenvector alignment or cluster correspondence. The framework also models CoT trajectories as transitions among latent semantic states, and SARE-based features match or outperform output-based confidence baselines in most settings.

rss · ArXiv CS.AI · Aug 3, 04:00

**Background**: Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting elicits reasoning in large language models by breaking down complex problems into intermediate steps. Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) is a kernel-based similarity metric used to compare neural network representations, often between layers or models. Existing interpretability methods for CoT often rely on output-level signals or collapse processing depth into a single scalar, leaving step-wise effort opaque.

## [Validity Audit Reveals Flaws in Agent-Safety Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28685) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A new paper audits four agent-safety benchmarks (R-Judge, InjecAgent, AgentHarm, AgentDojo) and finds that their scores are not comparable, with small model panels producing misleading correlations. The study shows that an 'always positive' policy can achieve an F1 score of 0.690 on R-Judge, outperforming five of 21 discriminating models. This work challenges the reliability of current agent-safety evaluations, which are widely used to assess AI safety. It highlights that benchmark scores are often quoted interchangeably despite measuring different behaviors, potentially leading to incorrect conclusions about model safety and hindering progress in AI safety research. The study ran each benchmark under its official implementation on up to 22 models, with MMLU and GPQA measured under one protocol. It found that R-Judge specificity against AgentHarm safety correlates -0.64 at n=7 but +0.02 at n=18, and a quarter of random size-7 subsets reach |ρ| ≥ 0.5 around that near-zero value. Capability predicts task success (ρ=+0.60) but correlates negatively with misalignment safety (ρ=-0.44, n=21).

rss · ArXiv CS.AI · Aug 3, 04:00

**Background**: Agent-safety benchmarks are designed to evaluate the safety of AI agents, which are systems that can perform tasks autonomously. These benchmarks measure different aspects of safety, such as resistance to prompt injection attacks (InjecAgent), harmfulness of agent behavior (AgentHarm), and safety risk awareness (R-Judge). However, the validity of these benchmarks as measurements has not been thoroughly examined, and this paper aims to audit their reliability.
